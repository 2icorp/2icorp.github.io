Storage Cost & Durability

Have you ever measured whether the data you must keep for ten years is still readable?

Korean commercial law requires ten years of accounting records. Medical law requires ten years of patient charts. Public records can require permanent retention, and the statute explicitly demands accurate and reliable reproduction. In most sites that data sits in the most expensive storage class, and nobody has ever checked that it still reads back. Cost and integrity are two faces of the same problem. We split the work three ways. Decide: break the bill down by cause and judge, per workload, where it should live. Measure: re-test vendor claims with standard tools. Build: a minimal configuration for data that has to stay air-gapped. Every number below was produced by code in a public repository and can be reproduced with one command.

Proven · measured, reproducible from public code Ready · method in hand, re-measured on your data Concept · an idea to validate together
83.7 USD/TB break-even Decide · settle the on-prem question with a number "The cloud is expensive, let's bring it home" rests entirely on one input: the price of a disk. Our model exposes that price as a variable and computes where the answer flips. In the synthetic case the recommendation flipped from on-prem back to cloud above 83.7 USD per raw TB, which is 2.6x the baseline. Since 2025 AI demand has kept disk and NAND supply tight and hardware prices rising, so skipping this axis means regretting the move three years later. Measured on synthetic data · a test enforces the flip exists
4.0x worse than doing nothing Decide · when "just move it to archive" is wrong We applied a day-30 deep-archive transition to a workload of 64KB objects retained for only 90 days. Storage cost fell, but per-object transition requests dominated, and a 180-day minimum duration against 90-day retention added early-delete penalties. The result cost four times more than doing nothing. The same model shows lifecycle winning for large objects. This is an area that needs calculation, not a rule of thumb. Measured on synthetic data · transition fees and penalties included
54.2% of the bill attributed to a cause Decide · the unexplained 45.8 percent is not claimed as explained We decompose a bill by cause rather than by line item: cold data left in a hot class, snapshot sprawl, egress, small-object request fees, over-provisioned volumes. In the synthetic case about half the bill resolved into addressable savings and the rest is left explicitly unattributed. Causes where acting would lose money are shown as such. Measured on synthetic data · double-counting blocked by test
30.0% diagnostic coverage Measure · coverage matters more than the score We diagnose a live deployment against 40 table-stakes storage capabilities. In the worked example we actually measured 12 of 40, which is 28.2 percent. The other 28 are reported as not measured, never as passing. Most diagnostic reports give you a score and stay silent about what was never tested. We put that number at the top of the table. Measured · against a 4-drive erasure-coded MinIO deployment
27/40 items that cannot pass at all Measure · code prevents unmeasured items from being reported as passing Items we lack the access or tooling to test have no code path to a passing state. Constructing a pass without an evidence payload raises an exception. Turning a failure into "not measured" to raise the score is recorded as a regression. It is the safety catch the diagnostic tool applies to itself, and it is the whole product. Measured · a test runs all 27 and asserts not-measured
1 critical finding in the worked example Measure · things you only learn by breaking them We delete shards, flip bits, try to delete objects under retention lock, and authenticate with the wrong secret. On the example target, missing in-flight encryption came back as a critical failure. Conversely, where absence of plaintext cannot prove encryption, the result is not-measured rather than a pass. Measured · includes 10 S3 sub-checks and 4 access-control checks
100% / 0% bitrot detection, checksums on and off Build · three replicas is not durability by itself We flipped a single bit in data on disk. With checksums, detection was 100 percent. Without them, zero. What is missed becomes silent corruption: the read succeeds and the content is wrong, which is the worst outcome a storage system can produce. Holding three copies and knowing those three copies are intact are different problems. Measured · single-bit flip at a random offset, headers not avoided
13.3% loss after six rounds without scrub Build · corruption accumulates, and then it is unrecoverable Injecting identical corruption in identical order, with background scrub as the only variable: the scrubbing arm repaired all 144 injuries and lost nothing, while the other lost 13.3 percent of objects within six rounds. Once a second replica rots while the first is already rotten, there is nothing to recover from. That is why "we have backups" and "we can restore" are different statements. Measured · 144 identical injuries, scrub as the only variable
2x usable capacity on the same disks Build · cheap hardware does not help if you lose it back in efficiency Tolerating the same two simultaneous losses, we put 1.45 GB of real model weights through both. Three-way replication writes 4.58 GB to disk; erasure coding writes 2.29 GB. Exactly double. These are not synthetic bytes: the store ran as six pods on Kubernetes and we measured what actually landed on disk. The 0.3 percentage-point gap from theory is the checksum header. Money saved on cheaper hardware disappears if half the capacity goes with it. Measured · real model weights, equal fault tolerance
4 defects found while every suite was green Verify · a pass rate does not mean it works With our own 40-item harness, 205 cases of the S3 conformance suite and our unit tests all passing, we deployed the store as pods on Kubernetes and attached production clients. The gateway died within a minute, and three more defects surfaced over the next few hours. All four came from two axes we had never tested along: feature combinations, and clients we did not write. A hundred more cases on the same axis would have found none of them. We published every defect and the reason our tests could not see it. Measured · four defects, each with the reason it went unseen
311 shards, 6s vLLM streaming a model straight out of this store Verify · a real serving stack actually uses it On a GPU node, vLLM used our store as its backing store, streamed the model and served inference. 311 safetensors shards in 6 seconds; model loading took 7.96 seconds. We also fed it training checkpoints: 20 of them, 4.03 GB total, at 117 MB/s, while force-deleting a storage node mid-run. Zero write failures, all 20 checksums matched, and training resumed from the last checkpoint. Measured · GPU serving and training checkpoints, node killed under load
50.2 → 0% replicas sharing one rack Build · so a single rack failure does not take them together Picking the top-scoring nodes while ignoring failure domains puts two or more replicas of half the chunks in a single domain. Lose power to one rack and all of that disappears at once. Domain-aware placement drives the figure to zero, and when domains are insufficient it refuses explicitly rather than quietly reducing redundancy. Measured · 12 nodes, 4 domains, 5000 chunks
15.4 → 30.0% coverage our store, self-diagnosis, all four points kept We pointed the tool at ourselves first, lost, and only then fixed it We ran our own diagnostic harness against the store we built. It came back at 15.4 percent coverage, 41.7 points, one critical failure. We put the sentence about losing at the top of the public repository before we changed anything. Then we fixed the store without touching a single line of the harness and reached 85.7 points with zero critical failures. Next we generalised a discovery defect in the harness itself, which widened coverage to 28.2 percent and pushed the score down to 77.3, because a storage-at-rest encryption failure that had been hiding came into the denominator. Read the score alone and that looks like a regression; read it with coverage and it is progress. We added a conditional-write item to the checklist, taking it to 40 items, and re-measured both sides: 79.2 points for us against 75.0 for MinIO. Stopping there would have let us claim a win. Instead we compared item by item and found the two runs identical on everything except transport encryption, and that one item was not a MinIO defect. It was our own harness running MinIO over plaintext HTTP. MinIO supports TLS fully. We brought the comparison target up the same way we bring up our own and measured again. All 12 measured items now match exactly: 30.0 percent coverage and 83.3 points on both sides, zero critical failures. So we do not write that we are better. What this harness actually says is that a 7,000-line reference implementation matches MinIO on the 12 items anyone can measure, and that the remaining 70 percent went unmeasured for both. All five reports are in the repository. Measured · both sides re-measured whenever an item is added
4 / 4 on both sides the item we added, passed by the other side too Evidence that we did not bend the harness toward ourselves Our checklist had no item for conditional writes, the feature that stops two clients writing the same key from silently overwriting each other. The place to measure it was simply empty, so we added it and the list went to 40 items. But adding an item for a feature we had just implemented looks self-serving, so we held to one rule: whenever an item is added, the other side gets re-measured on the same harness. Both sides passed all four checks. Had we tilted the harness this is where it would have shown, and it did not; the score gap still comes from exactly the same two items as before. Measured · 40 items, identical conditions for us and MinIO
33.3 → 66.6% usable capacity you can actually use at the same tolerance Twice the data on the same disks Three-way replication means buying three disks and using one. Hold the tolerance constant at two simultaneous losses, switch to erasure coding, and the same disks hold twice as much. We put a 256KiB object through both paths on one instance and measured what actually landed on disk: 786,480 bytes against 393,324 bytes, a 50.0 percent reduction. That is measured with framing and padding included, not a theoretical ratio. The tradeoff is repair: rebuilding one shard means reading several, so recovery costs more. We measure that cost and publish it too. Measured · 256KiB object, identical loss tolerance
90.7% s3-tests pass rate within our declared scope Compatibility is a pass rate against a standard suite, not an impression We ran the conformance suite maintained by Ceph against our gateway. Within the surface we declare we implement: 97 passed, 10 failed. The selection expression is published in the README so the scope is reproducible, and the unfiltered figure across everything including what we say we do not implement (205 of 838) is published too. The suite caught five listing bugs our own tests had missed. Measured · ceph/s3-tests at a pinned commit
732 → 99ms client p99 during rebalance What your users feel while data is moving Adding a node moves data. How much moves is arithmetic, but what users experience while it moves has to be measured. Running the same move plan with throttling as the only variable, p99 latency jumped from an 82 ms baseline to 732 ms unthrottled, and returned to 99 ms with throttling on. The cost is a rebalance that takes 6.1 times longer. We also note that the p99 of the arm with fewer samples is noisy. Measured · identical 209-move plan, both arms

To be honest: the figures above come from synthetic data and our own lab, and they have to be re-measured in your environment. Here is what we did not do. No formal consistency verification of the Jepsen kind. Multi-site replication and large-node operation are outside what we tested. The store we built is not a Ceph alternative but a minimal implementation for small air-gapped deployments, and that limit is written on the first screen of its repository. Every published price in the cost model ships marked unverified, and a customer-facing report will not generate while that mark stands. Published sources disagree on Seoul-region egress alone, so we re-check and pin the numbers at proposal time.

Air-gapped Transfer · AIR-GAP DATA DIODE

Even for equipment that can't be plugged into the internet,
we push models in one direction.

Air-gapped equipment with no network connection struggles to keep its AI models current. USB is the number-one intrusion path, so blocking it leaves on-site AI stuck running stale models. We stream files in one direction only, encoded as fountain codes that flow from a screen to a camera, so that even if more than half the fragments are lost, the remaining fragments alone reconstruct the original. A bandwidth-free reverse signal refills only the missing fragments for full recovery, but since bulk data still cannot travel back out, the diode's strictly one-way property holds.
